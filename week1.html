Scarcity & Core Concepts

Big Idea: Economics is the study of how individuals, businesses, and governments allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants and needs. Because we cannot have everything, we must choose — and every choice has a cost.

The Three Fundamental Questions

QuestionMeaningExample
WHAT to produce?Which goods and services should be made?Cars or hospitals?
HOW to produce?Which resources and methods to use?Labour-intensive or automated?
FOR WHOM?Who receives the goods produced?By price, need, or lottery?

Opportunity Cost

The value of the next-best alternative sacrificed when a decision is made. It captures what you give up — not just what you spend.

Formula
Opportunity Cost = Value of Next-Best Alternative Foregone

Example: You spend an evening studying instead of working a shift. Your opportunity cost is the income from the shift you skipped.

PPF (Production Possibilities Frontier): Points ON the curve = efficient. Points INSIDE = inefficient (resources wasted). Points OUTSIDE = currently unattainable.

Economic vs Accounting Profit

ConceptFormulaIncludes
Accounting ProfitRevenue − Explicit CostsOnly cash/visible costs
Economic ProfitRevenue − Explicit − Implicit CostsAll costs including foregone alternatives
Normal ProfitEconomic Profit = 0Firm covers all costs; competitive market outcome
Key Formula
Economic Profit = Total Revenue − Explicit Costs − Implicit Costs

Other Core Concepts

ConceptDefinition
Thinking at the MarginEvaluating the impact of producing or consuming ONE more unit
Ceteris ParibusAll other variables held constant during analysis
Positive EconomicsFactual, testable statements about how the world IS — e.g. "Unemployment is 5%"
Normative EconomicsValue judgements about how the world SHOULD be — e.g. "Unemployment should be lower"
SustainabilityUsing resources responsibly for current and future generations

Remember: Positive economics = FACTS. Normative economics = OPINIONS. Always identify which type a statement is before analysing it.

Demand — What Buyers Do

Definition: Demand is the quantity of a good or service that buyers are willing AND able to purchase at various prices, within a given time period. Demand only counts when backed by purchasing power (effective demand).

Law of Demand

As price rises → quantity demanded falls. As price falls → quantity demanded rises. This is an inverse relationship.

ReasonExplanation
Substitution EffectWhen price falls, consumers switch from more expensive alternatives
Income EffectA lower price increases consumers' real purchasing power
Diminishing Marginal UtilityEach extra unit consumed delivers less satisfaction; consumers pay less for additional units

Movements vs Shifts

Contraction of DemandPrice RISESMovement up-left along curve

Non-Price Determinants (Shift the Curve)

DeterminantDemand Increases When…

Number of BuyersMore consumers enter the market
Tastes & PreferencesTrend or fashion favours the good
ExpectationsConsumers expect future prices to rise
Price of SubstitutesPrice of a substitute good rises
Price of ComplementsPrice of a complementary good falls

Supply — What Producers Do

Definition: Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at various prices, within a given time period. The profit motive drives firms to produce more at higher prices.

Law of Supply

As price rises → quantity supplied rises. As price falls → quantity supplied falls. This is a direct (positive) relationship.

Factors That Shift the Supply Curve

FactorEffect on Supply
Production costs fallSupply increases (shifts right)
New production technologySupply increases
New producers enter the marketMarket supply increases
Favourable weather (agriculture)Supply increases
Government subsidySupply increases
Government taxSupply decreases (shifts left)
Input prices riseSupply decreases

Market Equilibrium

Definition: Market equilibrium occurs where supply and demand curves intersect. At this price, quantity supplied = quantity demanded, and there is no pressure for price to change.

Disequilibrium

SituationConditionMarket PressureResult
Surplus (Excess Supply)Qs > QdDownward pressure on pricePrice falls until equilibrium
Shortage (Excess Demand)Qd > QsUpward pressure on pricePrice rises until equilibrium

Price Controls

ControlSet Relative to EquilibriumEffect
Price Ceiling (binding)BELOW equilibriumCreates SHORTAGE
Price Ceiling (non-binding)ABOVE equilibriumNo effect
Price Floor (binding)ABOVE equilibriumCreates SURPLUS
Price Floor (non-binding)BELOW equilibriumNo effect

Examples: Price Ceiling → Rent controls create housing shortages. Price Floor → Minimum wage above market wage creates unemployment (labour surplus).

Trade — Absolute & Comparative Advantage

Why Trade? Even if one country is better at producing everything, trade still benefits both parties — as long as each specialises in what it produces at the lowest opportunity cost.

Absolute vs Comparative Advantage

ConceptDefinitionKey Question
Absolute AdvantageProduce MORE output per unit of input than another producerWho produces the most?
Comparative AdvantageProduce at a LOWER OPPORTUNITY COST than another producerWho gives up the least?

Worked Example — Town A vs Town B

Milk (litres/hr)Bread (loaves/hr)
Town A10025
Town B2010

Opportunity cost of 1 loaf of bread: Town A = 4 litres of milk. Town B = 2 litres of milk.

Comparative advantage: Town B in bread (lower OC). Town A in milk (lower OC).

Trade price range for bread: Must trade between 2 and 4 litres of milk — between each party's opportunity cost.

Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

MicroeconomicsMacroeconomics
FocusIndividual markets, firms, and consumersEntire national or global economy
Key VariablesPrice, output, firm costs, market structureGDP, inflation, unemployment, interest rates
Policy ToolsRegulation, market designFiscal & monetary policy
ExampleWhy did petrol prices rise this week?What caused the 2008 financial crisis?

Remember: Micro = TREES. Macro = FOREST. Both are needed to understand the full economy.

Economic Shocks

Definition: An economic shock is an unexpected event that significantly impacts the economy — causing sudden changes in prices, output, demand, or supply.

Type of ShockWhat Causes ItGraph EffectExample
Demand Shock (positive)Surge in consumer spendingD curve shifts RIGHTCOVID stimulus payments
Demand Shock (negative)Loss of consumer confidenceD curve shifts LEFT2008 financial crisis
Supply Shock (positive)Technology breakthroughS curve shifts RIGHTFracking lowering energy costs
Supply Shock (negative)Natural disaster / warS curve shifts LEFT2022 Ukraine war & wheat prices

Expected ShockUnexpected Shock
