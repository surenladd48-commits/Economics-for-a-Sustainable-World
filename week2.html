Microeconomic Fundamentals

Economics is about balancing profit maximisation with sustainable resource management. Microeconomics focuses on the individual and firm level; macroeconomics looks at the national and international level.

Supply and Demand as Core Models

Supply and demand curves are the fundamental models for understanding how markets work. This week we build on those foundations to examine how responsive buyers and sellers are to price changes — the concept of elasticity.

2

The Concept of Elasticity

Definition: Elasticity measures how much one variable changes in response to a change in another variable. It is about responsiveness.

The Rubber Band Analogy

Think of a rubber band: a highly elastic band stretches a lot in response to a small pull. A highly inelastic band barely stretches at all. Markets work the same way — some respond dramatically to price changes, others barely move.

General Formula
Elasticity (e) = % Change in Quantity ÷ % Change in Price

Four Types of Elasticity

TypeMeasures
Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)How much quantity demanded changes when price changes
Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)How much quantity supplied changes when price changes
Cross-Price Elasticity of DemandHow much demand for Good A changes when price of Good B changes
Income Elasticity of DemandHow much quantity demanded changes when consumer income changes

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

Definition: PED measures the responsiveness of demand to a change in price.

PED Formula
PED = % Change in Quantity Demanded ÷ % Change in Price

Types of Demand

PED > 1
Elastic
Highly sensitive to price — luxury goods
PED < 1
Inelastic
Less sensitive — necessities, addictive goods
PED = 1
Unit Elastic
% change in Qd = % change in price
PED = 0
Perfectly Inelastic
Demand doesn't change at all
PED = ∞
Perfectly Elastic
Demand is infinitely responsive

Factors Affecting PED

FactorEffect on PED

Price Relative to IncomeHigher % of income → more elastic
Cost of SwitchingHigh switching costs → inelastic demand
Brand LoyaltyStrong brand loyalty → inelastic demand
Necessity vs LuxuryNecessities → inelastic; Luxuries → elastic

Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

Definition: PES measures the responsiveness of supply to a change in price.

PES Formula
PES = % Change in Quantity Supplied ÷ % Change in Price

Types of Supply

PES > 1
Elastic
Supply highly responsive to price
PES < 1
Inelastic
Supply not very responsive
PES = 0
Perfectly Inelastic
Supply fixed regardless of price
PES = ∞
Perfectly Elastic
Supply can change without any price change

Factors Affecting PES

FactorEffect on PES
Spare CapacityMore spare capacity → more elastic supply
Stock LevelsHigh stock levels → more elastic supply
Production TimeShorter production times → more elastic supply
Ease of Factor SubstitutionHigh ease → more elastic supply

Midpoint Formula for Elasticity

Purpose: To calculate percentage changes in a consistent way when moving between two points on a curve. It avoids the problem of getting different answers depending on which direction you measure.

Midpoint Formula — Step by Step
ΔQ = Q2 − Q1     ΔP = P2 − P1
Qavg = (Q1 + Q2) ÷ 2     Pavg = (P1 + P2) ÷ 2
% ΔQ = (ΔQ ÷ Qavg) × 100%
% ΔP = (ΔP ÷ Pavg) × 100%
Elasticity = % ΔQ ÷ % ΔP

Worked Example 1 — PED Calculation

Price falls from $10 → $8. Quantity demanded rises from 100 → 130.

  • ΔQ = 130 − 100 = 30
  • ΔP = 8 − 10 = −2
  • Qavg = (100 + 130) ÷ 2 = 115
  • Pavg = (10 + 8) ÷ 2 = 9
  • % ΔQ = (30 ÷ 115) × 100 ≈ 26.09%
  • % ΔP = (−2 ÷ 9) × 100 ≈ −22.22%
  • PED = 26.09 ÷ 22.22 ≈ 1.17 → Elastic (PED > 1)

Worked Example 2 — PES Calculation

Price rises from $20 → $22. Quantity supplied rises from 200 → 210.

  • ΔQ = 210 − 200 = 10
  • ΔP = 22 − 20 = 2
  • Qavg = (200 + 210) ÷ 2 = 205
  • Pavg = (20 + 22) ÷ 2 = 21
  • % ΔQ = (10 ÷ 205) × 100 ≈ 4.88%
  • % ΔP = (2 ÷ 21) × 100 ≈ 9.52%
  • PES = 4.88 ÷ 9.52 ≈ 0.51 → Inelastic (PES < 1)

Elasticity, Taxation & Tax Incidence

Tax Wedge & Tax Revenue

Tax Wedge: The difference between the price consumers pay and the price producers receive after a tax is imposed.

Tax Revenue: Tax per unit × quantity sold after the tax.

Effect: Taxes shift the supply curve upward, leading to a new equilibrium with a higher consumer price and lower producer price — and lower quantity traded.

Who Bears the Tax Burden?

Key Rule: The tax burden falls more heavily on the side of the market with the more inelastic curve.

SituationWho Bears More of the Tax?Why?
Inelastic DemandConsumersThey don't reduce consumption much when price rises
Elastic DemandProducersProducers can't pass tax on without losing too many sales
Inelastic SupplyProducersThey can't easily reduce supply in response to price fall
Elastic SupplyConsumersProducers easily redirect resources; consumers absorb the tax

Policy Discussion — Which Goods Should Governments Tax?

  • Maximum tax revenue: Tax goods with inelastic demand (e.g. petrol, tobacco, alcohol) — quantity sold doesn't fall much.
  • Fairness of tax incidence: Consider who bears the burden — taxing inelastic goods hits lower-income consumers harder (regressive).
  • Alternatives to taxes: Subsidies (encourage desired behaviour), regulations (cap harmful activity), tradeable permits — all avoid deadweight loss of taxation.
7

Welfare Economics — Surplus & Deadweight Loss

Consumer Surplus

The additional benefit consumers receive when they buy a good for less than they were willing to pay. On a graph: the triangular area between the demand curve and the actual market price.

Producer Surplus

The additional profit producers earn when they sell a good for more than their minimum acceptable price. On a graph: the triangular area between the supply curve and the actual market price.

Deadweight Loss (DWL)

The reduction in total economic surplus that occurs when a market is not operating efficiently — caused by taxes, price controls, or other market distortions. DWL represents trades that would have benefited both parties but did not occur.

Smartphone example: Market price = $800. Consumers willing to pay up to $1,000. Consumer surplus = ($1,000 − $800) × quantity sold. If demand rises and price increases to $900, consumer surplus increases. If supply falls and price rises to $1,100, consumer surplus decreases.

How Shifts Affect Surplus

EventConsumer SurplusProducer Surplus
Increase in DemandIncreases (more consumers benefit)Increases (higher prices)
Decrease in DemandMay increase further (lower prices)Decreases (lower prices)
Increase in SupplyIncreases (lower prices)May decrease (prices fall)
Decrease in SupplyDecreases (higher prices)May increase (higher prices)
